What we are betting on

Execution is now frictionless.
Judgement is what's scarce.

A year ago, shipping a working web app might have taken a team several months. Today, a single person with Claude or Cursor can draft one in a weekend. The same collapse is happening across design, video, music, research, and writing. The trajectory is still accelerating.

So the old question holds. Not what can we build, but what should we build.

As execution becomes frictionless, the scarce skill is increasingly judgement. The capacity to notice what matters before building anything. The rigour to ask what something costs, and who bears that cost. Underneath both, the instinct that insists a particular thing needs to exist.

These are often called soft skills, though they may be the hardest ones to develop in a moment when the tools keep getting easier. They are also, we think, the skills a school curriculum now needs to centre.

"Not what can we build. What should we build."

Your students are entering an AI-exposed economy carrying a script that worked for their parents. Do well on the tests. Take the reliable path. The system will hold you. That story is beginning to loosen, and the reliable paths are being reshaped faster than any curriculum can track.

The need to know what you are for will not change. To notice what only you, from where you stand, can see.

C-Levels is the small, deliberate version of that, for schools.

How it works

The 4Cs.

Curiosity becomes learning when it has a path. The whole loop runs in 1 to 2 days.

Creation

What can you build from what you found?

Students draw on AI, primary sources, articles, and worked examples to investigate, then turn what they find into something concrete. The format follows the pursuit.

  • ↓ A theory

04

Communication

Can someone else understand it?

Students explain their work to someone who knows little about the topic. Vague thinking gets exposed. So does the moment a student realises they actually understand what they made.

"This was my question. This is what I found. This is what changed my mind. This is what I made. This is what I still want to know."

Responsible AI

AI is part of the pursuit.
It does not get to do the pursuing.

Answers from a model are treated as material to inspect, not conclusions to repeat. AI use is not hidden in C-Levels. What we make visible is the thinking around it.

A.

Students ask stronger follow-up questions instead of accepting the first response.

B.

Compare what the model says against primary sources, and write down where they disagree.

C.

Spot the assumptions a model is making, and check what is missing from its answer.

D.

Separate evidence from confidence. A confident answer is not a correct one.

E.

Explain the final work in the student's own words, without the screen open.

Questions

Worth asking before
the meeting.

You have not run this yet. Why should we be your first?

add

Because the pilot is free, fully documented, and the school keeps everything that comes out of it. The honest exchange is: you give us the chance to test the design with real students, we give you a finished programme and a case study you can use. If something does not work, you keep what we learned and we walk away with the lesson. Either side can pull out before the run.

What does this cost after the pilot?

add

The pricing for paid cohorts will be set after the pilot, based on what we learn. We will not commit a school to a number while we are still proving the format. The two pilot schools will see the proposed pricing first.

Do students need any prior AI exposure?

add

No. The programme is designed for students who have used ChatGPT casually and for students who have never opened it. The work is on the asking and the explaining, not the prompting.

Which AI tools do you use?

add

Claude, NotebookLM, and search. Tools the school is comfortable with take precedence. We adapt to whatever device and platform rules are already in place.

How is this different from an AI workshop?

add

Most AI workshops are about the tool. C-Levels is about the inquiry behind the tool. The student leaves with a completed pursuit and a record of how they got there. The deliverable is not a prompt library. It is a thinking artefact.
